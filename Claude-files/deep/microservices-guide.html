2. Monolithic Architecture Explained (with Diagram)

2.1 What Is a Monolith?

A monolithic application is built and deployed as one single unit — all modules (accounts, cards, loans, notifications, reporting) live in one codebase, share one database, compile into one WAR/JAR, and are deployed together on one (or a few identical) application server instances.

3. Microservices Architecture Explained (with Diagram)

3.1 What Are Microservices?

Microservices architecture splits an application into multiple small, independently deployable services, each owning its own business capability, its own codebase, its own database, and its own deployment lifecycle — services communicate over the network (REST, gRPC, messaging) instead of in-process method calls.

3.3 Key Characteristics of Microservices

  • Independently deployable — deploying Cards Service doesn't require redeploying Accounts or Loans.
  • Own data store — each service owns its schema/database, no other service touches it directly (Database per Service pattern — Section 27).
  • Communicate over the network — REST, gRPC, or asynchronous messaging (Kafka/RabbitMQ), never direct in-process calls.
  • Organized around business capability — "Accounts," "Cards," "Loans" are business domains, not technical layers.
  • Decentralized governance — different services can (in theory) use different languages/databases, though most Java shops keep it consistent with Spring Boot across services.
  • Built for failure — resilience patterns (circuit breaker, retry, bulkhead) assume any service call might fail.

5. Advantages & Disadvantages of Monolithic Architecture

5.1 Advantages

  • Simple to develop, test, and deploy initially — one build, one deployable artifact.
  • Easier debugging — a single stack trace across the whole request, no network hops to trace.
  • No distributed system complexity — no service discovery, no network latency between modules, no partial failure handling.
  • Simpler transactions — a single database means ACID transactions across modules are trivial.
  • Lower operational overhead — one thing to monitor, one thing to deploy, one log file.
  • Good fit for small teams and early-stage products/startups where speed of initial delivery matters more than independent scaling.

5.2 Disadvantages

  • Scaling is all-or-nothing — you can't scale just the "Cards" module without scaling the entire app.
  • Slower release cycles — a tiny change in one module requires rebuilding/retesting/redeploying everything.
  • Technology lock-in — hard to adopt a new framework/language for just one part of the system.
  • Team bottleneck — many developers committing to the same codebase increases merge conflicts and coordination overhead as the team grows.
  • Larger blast radius for bugs — a memory leak or bug in one module can crash the entire application, including unrelated modules.
  • Harder to onboard new developers as the codebase grows very large over years.

6. Advantages & Disadvantages of Microservices Architecture

6.1 Advantages

  • Independent scaling — scale only the service experiencing load (e.g., Loans Service during month-end processing).
  • Independent deployment — release Cards Service ten times a day without touching Accounts or Loans.
  • Fault isolation — with circuit breakers, a failing Loans Service doesn't necessarily take down Accounts or Cards.
  • Technology flexibility — teams can choose the best database/framework per service's needs.
  • Team autonomy — small teams own a service end-to-end ("you build it, you run it"), improving ownership and velocity.
  • Easier to understand a single service in isolation, once you know the boundaries.

6.2 Disadvantages

  • Distributed system complexity — network latency, partial failures, eventual consistency all become real concerns.
  • Operational overhead — needs service discovery, API gateway, centralized logging, distributed tracing, container orchestration.
  • Data consistency is hard — no more simple cross-module ACID transactions; requires Sagas/eventual consistency (Section 22).
  • Testing is harder — end-to-end tests span multiple services/network calls; needs contract testing and test environments per integration.
  • Debugging is harder — a single user request might touch 5 services; requires distributed tracing to follow the full path.
  • Higher infrastructure cost — many small JVMs/containers running 24/7 vs one larger one, more moving parts to secure and patch.
  • Requires DevOps maturity — CI/CD, containerization, and monitoring must all be solid before microservices pay off.
Interview tip: Microservices are not "free" — they trade development simplicity for operational flexibility and independent scaling. An organization without strong CI/CD, monitoring, and DevOps practices will often struggle more with microservices than they would with a well-organized monolith.

8.4 A Real Cross-Service Flow — "Close Account" Use Case

  1. Customer calls DELETE /accounts/{id} via the Gateway → routed to Accounts Service.
  2. Accounts Service checks: does this account have any active cards or open loans? It calls Cards Service and Loans Service (via Feign/REST) to confirm.
  3. If clear, Accounts Service marks the account closed and publishes an AccountClosedEvent to Kafka.
  4. Cards Service (Kafka consumer) automatically deactivates any lingering cards linked to that account.
  5. Loans Service (Kafka consumer) flags the customer's loan record as "account closed" for compliance reporting.
Why this matters: This single example touches synchronous REST calls (immediate validation), asynchronous events (Kafka, for eventual side effects), and shows why each service having its own database means cross-service checks must go through APIs/events — never direct SQL joins across services.

9. Migrating from Monolith to Microservices (Strangler Fig Pattern)

9.1 Why Not a "Big Bang" Rewrite?

Rewriting an entire monolith into microservices all at once is extremely risky — it freezes feature delivery for months/years, and by the time it's done, business requirements have often changed. Real organizations use the Strangler Fig Pattern: gradually carve out and replace pieces of the monolith while it keeps running in production.

The original monolith no longer exists — it has been fully "strangled" and replaced, one module at a time, with zero big-bang cutover risk.

9.3 Practical Migration Steps

  1. Identify bounded contexts using Domain-Driven Design — figure out natural seams (Accounts, Cards, Loans are separate business domains).
  2. Pick the least risky/most isolated module first (often Cards, since it has fewer cross-cutting dependencies than Accounts).
  3. Extract the module's database tables into a new schema/database dedicated to the new service.
  4. Build the new Spring Boot microservice, replicate the module's functionality, add integration tests comparing old vs new behavior.
  5. Introduce an API Gateway/router in front of both monolith and new service, routing by path.
  6. Dual-write or sync data temporarily if needed during transition (e.g., CDC — Change Data Capture — from old DB to new DB).
  7. Cut over traffic gradually (Canary-style — 5% → 50% → 100%) to the new service, monitoring error rates closely.
  8. Remove the old module's code and tables from the monolith once fully migrated and stable.
  9. Repeat for the next module (Loans, then Accounts) until the monolith is fully replaced.
Real scenario: "Our bank ran the old monolith and new Cards microservice side by side for six weeks, with the Gateway splitting traffic by customer ID hash — this let us validate the new service's correctness on real production traffic without risking a full cutover, and roll back instantly by just changing the routing rule if issues appeared."

12. API Gateway — Spring Cloud Gateway

12.1 Why an API Gateway?

Without a gateway, the frontend/mobile app would need to know the address of every microservice individually, handle auth in multiple places, and every service would need to implement rate limiting/CORS separately. The API Gateway is a single entry point that handles routing, authentication, rate limiting, and cross-cutting concerns once, centrally.

12.2 Gateway Responsibilities

  • Routing — forwards /accounts/** to Accounts Service, /cards/** to Cards Service, etc.
  • Authentication/Authorization — validates JWT tokens once at the edge (Section 33).
  • Rate limiting — protects backend services from being overwhelmed (Section 20).
  • Load balancing — spreads requests across healthy service instances (via Eureka integration).
  • Cross-cutting concerns — CORS, request logging, response header injection.

12.3 Real Gateway Configuration

# application.yml — api-gateway
spring:
  cloud:
    gateway:
      routes:
        - id: accounts-route
          uri: lb://ACCOUNTS-SERVICE       # lb:// = load-balanced via Eureka
          predicates:
            - Path=/accounts/**
          filters:
            - RewritePath=/accounts/(?<segment>.*), /$\{segment}
        - id: cards-route
          uri: lb://CARDS-SERVICE
          predicates:
            - Path=/cards/**
        - id: loans-route
          uri: lb://LOANS-SERVICE
          predicates:
            - Path=/loans/**

13. Inter-Service Communication — REST

13.1 Simple Synchronous REST Call

The most common way microservices talk directly to each other — a plain HTTP call, typically JSON over REST, using Spring's RestTemplate (older, being phased out) or the newer WebClient (reactive, recommended for new code).
@Service
public class AccountsService {

    private final WebClient webClient;

    public CardsDto getCardsForCustomer(Long customerId) {
        return webClient.get()
            .uri("http://CARDS-SERVICE/cards/customer/{id}", customerId)
            .retrieve()
            .bodyToMono(CardsDto.class)
            .block();   // blocking call — simplest to reason about for typical CRUD services
    }
}

14. Feign Client — Declarative REST Calls

14.1 Why Feign?

Feign lets you call another microservice as if it were a simple Java interface — no manual URL building, no manual JSON parsing. Spring Cloud OpenFeign integrates directly with Eureka for service discovery and load balancing.

14.2 Real Feign Client Example

@FeignClient(name = "cards-service")   // "cards-service" resolved via Eureka automatically
public interface CardsFeignClient {

    @GetMapping("/cards/customer/{customerId}")
    ResponseEntity<CardsDto> getCardsByCustomer(@PathVariable Long customerId);
}

// Usage inside AccountsService
@Service
@RequiredArgsConstructor
public class AccountsService {
    private final CardsFeignClient cardsFeignClient;

    public boolean hasActiveCards(Long customerId) {
        ResponseEntity<CardsDto> response = cardsFeignClient.getCardsByCustomer(customerId);
        return response.getBody() != null && response.getBody().isActive();
    }
}

// Enable in the main application class
@SpringBootApplication
@EnableFeignClients
public class AccountsServiceApplication { ... }
Interview tip: Feign is built on top of the same HTTP mechanics as RestTemplate/WebClient — its value is purely developer ergonomics (declarative interfaces) plus seamless integration with Eureka load balancing and Resilience4j fallback annotations.

14.3 Feign with Resilience4j Fallback

@FeignClient(name = "cards-service", fallback = CardsFeignFallback.class)
public interface CardsFeignClient { ... }

@Component
public class CardsFeignFallback implements CardsFeignClient {
    @Override
    public ResponseEntity<CardsDto> getCardsByCustomer(Long customerId) {
        return ResponseEntity.ok(CardsDto.empty());   // graceful degradation when Cards Service is down
    }
}

15. gRPC Basics for Microservices

15.1 What Is gRPC?

gRPC is a high-performance RPC (Remote Procedure Call) framework using Protocol Buffers (binary format) instead of JSON, and HTTP/2 instead of HTTP/1.1 — significantly faster and more compact than REST for high-throughput internal service-to-service calls.

15.2 Simple .proto Definition

syntax = "proto3";

service LoansService {
  rpc GetLoanDetails (LoanRequest) returns (LoanResponse);
}

message LoanRequest {
  int64 loanId = 1;
}

message LoanResponse {
  int64 loanId = 1;
  double outstandingAmount = 2;
  string status = 3;
}

15.3 REST vs gRPC — When to Use Which

PerformanceSlower (text-based, HTTP/1.1 typical)Much faster (binary, HTTP/2 multiplexing)
Human readabilityHigh — easy to inspect/debugLow — binary, needs tooling to inspect
Browser supportNativeNeeds grpc-web proxy for browsers

Real-world note: Most Java shops use REST/Feign as the default for microservice communication and reserve gRPC for specific high-throughput internal paths (e.g., a fraud-scoring service called on every transaction) where the performance gain is worth the added tooling complexity.

16. Load Balancing (Client-Side & Server-Side)

16.1 Server-Side Load Balancing

A dedicated load balancer (Azure Load Balancer, NGINX, Kubernetes Service) sits in front of service instances and distributes incoming requests — the caller doesn't know or care how many instances exist.

16.2 Client-Side Load Balancing (Spring Cloud LoadBalancer)

The calling service itself (via Eureka's list of healthy instances) picks which instance to call — used by Feign/WebClient with lb:// URIs. This avoids needing a separate load balancer hop for internal service-to-service calls.

→
Gets list: [10.0.1.5:8082, 10.0.1.9:8082, 10.0.1.14:8082]
→
Spring Cloud LoadBalancer picks one (round-robin by default)

18. Circuit Breaker Pattern — Resilience4j

18.1 The Problem — Cascading Failures

If Cards Service becomes slow/unresponsive, and Accounts Service keeps calling it and waiting on every request, Accounts Service's own threads get exhausted waiting — eventually Accounts Service becomes unresponsive too. One slow service can cascade into a total system outage.

18.2 How a Circuit Breaker Works

Circuit Breaker States
CLOSED
(calls flow normally)
→ failure threshold exceeded →

18.3 Resilience4j Circuit Breaker — Real Code

@Service
public class AccountsService {

    @CircuitBreaker(name = "cardsService", fallbackMethod = "cardsFallback")
    public CardsDto getCards(Long customerId) {
        return cardsFeignClient.getCardsByCustomer(customerId).getBody();
    }

    public CardsDto cardsFallback(Long customerId, Throwable t) {
        log.warn("Cards Service unavailable, returning fallback for customer {}", customerId);
        return CardsDto.empty();     // graceful degradation instead of full failure
    }
}

Interview tip: A Circuit Breaker doesn't prevent failures — it prevents a failing dependency from cascading into your own service by failing fast and giving the downstream service time to recover, while your own service stays responsive (even if in a degraded state).

19. Retry Pattern

19.1 When Retry Helps

Some failures are transient — a brief network blip, a momentary spike in load causing a timeout. Retrying the same call a few times, often with a growing delay (exponential backoff), can succeed without any real problem existing.

19.2 Resilience4j Retry — Real Code

@Retry(name = "cardsService", fallbackMethod = "cardsFallback")
@CircuitBreaker(name = "cardsService", fallbackMethod = "cardsFallback")
public CardsDto getCards(Long customerId) {
    return cardsFeignClient.getCardsByCustomer(customerId).getBody();
}

resilience4j:
  retry:
    instances:
      cardsService:
        max-attempts: 3
        wait-duration: 500ms
        exponential-backoff-multiplier: 2   # 500ms, 1000ms, 2000ms between attempts
        retry-exceptions:
          - java.io.IOException
          - java.util.concurrent.TimeoutException

19.3 Retry Danger — Non-Idempotent Operations

Retrying a "create payment" call blindly can create duplicate payments if the first attempt actually succeeded but the response was lost. Retry should only be applied to idempotent operations, or combined with an idempotency key (Section 25).

20. Bulkhead Pattern

20.1 The Ship Analogy

A ship is divided into watertight compartments ("bulkheads") — if one compartment floods, the rest of the ship stays afloat. The Bulkhead pattern applies the same idea to software: isolate resources (thread pools, connection pools) per dependency so one slow/failing dependency can't exhaust resources needed by unrelated calls.

20.2 Resilience4j Bulkhead — Real Code

@Bulkhead(name = "cardsService", type = Bulkhead.Type.THREADPOOL)
public CardsDto getCards(Long customerId) { ... }

resilience4j:
  thread-pool-bulkhead:
    instances:
      cardsService:
        max-thread-pool-size: 10
        core-thread-pool-size: 5
        queue-capacity: 20
Real scenario: "Before adding bulkheads, a slow Loans Service call was exhausting Accounts Service's entire shared HTTP thread pool, which also blocked unrelated Cards Service calls from getting a thread at all. Isolating each downstream call into its own bounded thread pool meant a slow Loans Service no longer starved Cards Service calls."

22. Distributed Transactions & the Saga Pattern

22.1 Why Traditional Transactions Don't Work Across Services

In a monolith, "create loan + deduct account balance" is one local ACID transaction. Across microservices with separate databases, there's no single transaction spanning both — a distributed 2-Phase-Commit (2PC) is possible but slow, fragile, and rarely used in modern microservices due to blocking/availability tradeoffs.

22.2 The Saga Pattern — Solution

A Saga breaks a business transaction into a sequence of local transactions, each in one service, with a compensating transaction defined for each step to undo it if a later step fails — trading strict ACID consistency for eventual consistency with guaranteed rollback logic.

22.3 Real Example — "Apply for Loan and Disburse to Account" Saga

1. Loans Service: create loan (PENDING)
→
2. Accounts Service: credit disbursed amount
→
3. Loans Service: mark loan ACTIVE
Loan Disbursement Saga — Failure & Compensation
1. Loans Service: create loan (PENDING)
→
2. Accounts Service: credit FAILS (account frozen)
↓ compensating transaction triggered
Loans Service: mark loan CANCELLED (undo step 1)

24. Event-Driven Architecture

24.1 Core Idea

Instead of services calling each other directly and waiting for a response, services publish events ("something happened") to a message broker, and other interested services subscribe and react independently and asynchronously.

24.2 Real Event Flow — Banking Example

→ publishes CardBlockedEvent →
Kafka Topic: card-events
↓ multiple independent consumers
Notification Service: sends SMS/email
Fraud Service: flags recent transactions for review
Analytics Service: updates dashboards

24.3 Benefits of Event-Driven Architecture

  • Services are decoupled — Cards Service doesn't need to know Notification/Fraud/Analytics services exist at all.
  • New consumers can be added later without changing the publisher.
  • Naturally resilient to a consumer being temporarily down — messages queue up and are processed once it recovers.
Trade-off: consistency becomes eventual, not immediate — for a brief window, Fraud Service may not yet know about a just-blocked card. Design must account for this (Section 25 — Idempotency, and careful UX messaging like "your request is being processed").

26. Idempotency in Microservices

26.1 What Idempotency Means

An operation is idempotent if performing it multiple times produces the same result as performing it once — critical in distributed systems because network retries, timeouts, and duplicate message delivery are normal, expected occurrences, not edge cases.

26.2 Real Example — Idempotency Key for Loan Disbursement

@PostMapping("/loans/disburse")
public ResponseEntity<DisbursementResponse> disburse(
        @RequestHeader("Idempotency-Key") String idempotencyKey,
        @RequestBody DisbursementRequest request) {

    Optional<Disbursement> existing = disbursementRepository.findByIdempotencyKey(idempotencyKey);
    if (existing.isPresent()) {
        return ResponseEntity.ok(toResponse(existing.get()));   // return the SAME result, don't re-disburse
    }

    Disbursement result = disbursementService.process(request, idempotencyKey);
    return ResponseEntity.ok(toResponse(result));
}
The client generates a unique Idempotency-Key per logical operation (e.g., a UUID stored client-side) and sends the exact same key on retries — the server recognizes the duplicate and returns the original result instead of processing it again.

26.3 Where Idempotency Matters Most

  • Payment/disbursement operations (never double-charge or double-credit).
  • Kafka consumers (a message can be delivered more than once — "at-least-once" delivery is the norm) — track processed message IDs to avoid double-processing.
  • Retry logic on any state-changing (non-GET) API call.

27. Database per Service Pattern

27.1 The Rule

Each microservice owns its own database/schema exclusively — no other service is allowed to directly query or modify another service's tables. All access to a service's data goes through its API (or its published events).
Database per Service — Banking Example
Accounts Service
owns accounts_db
Cards Service
owns cards_db
Loans Service
owns loans_db
Loans Service cannot run SELECT * FROM accounts_db.account directly — it must call Accounts Service's API to get account details.

27.2 Why This Rule Exists

  • Keeps services truly independently deployable — changing Accounts Service's schema doesn't break Loans Service, since Loans never touched those tables directly.
  • Preserves clear ownership and encapsulation of business logic/validation within the owning service.
  • Allows each service to pick the best-fit database technology (though most Java shops standardize on relational DBs for consistency).

27.3 The Cost — No More Cross-Service Joins

Building a report needing data from Accounts + Cards + Loans together can no longer be a simple SQL JOIN — it requires either API composition (call all three services and merge in code), a dedicated reporting/read database populated via events (see CQRS, Section 28), or a data warehouse fed by each service's data pipeline.

28. CQRS — Basic Understanding

28.1 What CQRS Means

CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation) separates the model used to write data (Commands) from the model used to read data (Queries) — they can even live in entirely different databases, optimized independently.

28.2 Real Example — Loan Reporting Dashboard

CQRS in the Banking Example
Loans Service (write model)
normalized relational tables
↓ publishes LoanCreated / LoanUpdated events
Kafka
↓ consumed and projected into
Reporting DB (read model)
denormalized, pre-joined for fast dashboard queries

28.3 Why Bother?

  • Write side stays clean/normalized for correctness; read side is optimized purely for fast queries (denormalized, indexed for dashboards).
  • Read load (dashboards, reports) never impacts the performance of the transactional write database.
  • Complexity cost is real — you now maintain two models and an event pipeline keeping them in sync; only use CQRS where the read/write patterns genuinely differ enough to justify it (e.g., heavy reporting needs), not for simple CRUD services.

31. Distributed Tracing — OpenTelemetry, Zipkin, Jaeger

31.1 The Problem Tracing Solves

Logs alone tell you what happened inside one service. Distributed tracing tells you the full journey of one request across all services it touched, with exact timing at each hop — essential for finding which service is the actual bottleneck in a slow multi-service call chain.

31.2 How It Works — Trace & Span

34. Security in Microservices — JWT, OAuth2, Keycloak

34.1 Why Security Is Harder in Microservices

In a monolith, authenticating once at the front door and using a server-side session is simple. Across many independently-scaled, stateless microservices, you need a token-based approach that any service can verify without a shared session store.

34.2 JWT (JSON Web Token) — How It Works

JWT Authentication Flow
Client logs in with username/password
→
Auth Server issues signed JWT
↓ client sends JWT in Authorization header on every request
API Gateway validates JWT signature + expiry
→
Forwards request (with claims) to Accounts/Cards/Loans Service
A JWT contains three parts — Header (algorithm), Payload (claims: user ID, roles, expiry), Signature (verifies it wasn't tampered with) — any service holding the public key/secret can verify it independently, with no need to call back to the auth server for every request.

  • Kubernetes' built-in DNS (cards-service.prod.svc.cluster.local) often complements or can replace Eureka on Kubernetes-native platforms — some organizations use both together during migration, others rely on Kubernetes Service discovery alone once fully containerized.

With many independently deployable services, each team needs a safe way to release their service frequently without risking the whole platform — these three strategies (fully detailed with diagrams in the companion CI/CD guide) are applied per-service.

39. Fault Tolerance & High Availability

39.1 What "High Availability" Really Means

HA means the system keeps serving requests correctly even when individual components fail — no single point of failure, redundancy at every layer (multiple pod replicas, multiple availability zones, replicated databases).

39.2 Layers of Fault Tolerance in Our Banking Example

Node/Zone levelPods spread across multiple Availability Zones via pod anti-affinity rules
Service-to-service callsCircuit Breaker + Retry + Bulkhead + Timeout (Resilience4j)

39.3 Timeout — The Most Underrated Resilience Pattern

resilience4j:
  timelimiter:
    instances:
      cardsService:
        timeout-duration: 2s   # never wait indefinitely on a downstream call
Without an explicit timeout, a hung downstream call can tie up caller threads indefinitely — always set a sane timeout on every inter-service call, tuned to realistic p99 latency plus margin.

40. How We Implement Microservices in Real-Time (End-to-End)

40.1 The Full Implementation Journey

  1. Domain analysis: identify bounded contexts using Domain-Driven Design workshops with business stakeholders (Accounts, Cards, Loans emerge as natural boundaries).
  2. Define APIs first (API-first design): write OpenAPI/Swagger contracts for each service before writing implementation code, so teams can work in parallel against agreed contracts.
  3. Scaffold infrastructure services: stand up Eureka, Config Server, and API Gateway first — every business microservice depends on these.
  4. Build each microservice: Spring Boot app with layered structure (Controller → Service → Repository), its own database, unit + integration tests (Testcontainers).
  5. Add resilience: Resilience4j circuit breaker/retry/bulkhead/timeout on every inter-service call from day one, not as an afterthought.
  6. Add observability: Actuator health endpoints, Micrometer metrics, OpenTelemetry tracing, structured JSON logging with correlation IDs — built in from the start.
  7. Add security: JWT validation via Keycloak/OAuth2 at the Gateway and enforced again at each service (defense in depth).
  8. Containerize: Dockerfile per service, docker-compose for local multi-service development.
  9. Set up CI/CD: one pipeline per service (Azure DevOps/Jenkins/GitHub Actions) — build, test, scan, containerize, deploy independently.
  10. Deploy to Kubernetes: namespaces per environment, HPA for autoscaling, Ingress for routing, monitored via Prometheus/Grafana.
  11. Iterate: add new microservices only when a genuine new bounded context emerges — resist splitting too finely too early ("nanoservices" anti-pattern).

40.2 Real Team Structure (Conway's Law in Action)

"Organizations design systems that mirror their own communication structure" (Conway's Law). Our bank organizes around the same three services: an Accounts team, a Cards team, and a Loans team, each owning their service's full lifecycle — code, database, on-call, deployment — with a shared Platform/DevOps team owning Eureka, Gateway, Config Server, and the Kubernetes cluster itself.

40.3 Communication Between Teams (Not Just Services)

  • API contracts as the interface between teams — changes to a shared API go through a review process (often using consumer-driven contract testing, e.g., Pact) so a breaking change is caught before it reaches production.
  • Shared platform standards — common logging format, common base Docker images, common Resilience4j default configs — maintained by the Platform team, adopted by all service teams.
  • Regular architecture sync — service teams meet periodically to discuss cross-service events/contracts, especially before introducing new Kafka topics or breaking API changes.

41. How to Maintain Microservices in Production (Real Organization Practices)

41.1 Day-2 Operations Checklist

  • On-call rotation per service team — the team that built Cards Service is paged when Cards Service alerts fire ("you build it, you run it").
  • Runbooks — documented step-by-step recovery procedures for common incidents (e.g., "Cards Service circuit breaker stuck open — check Card Network API status page first").
  • Dependency map kept up to date — a living diagram of which services call which, so impact analysis during incidents is fast.
  • Regular chaos testing — deliberately killing pods/injecting latency (Chaos Monkey-style) in non-prod to verify circuit breakers/retries actually work as designed.
  • Capacity planning — reviewing HPA scaling history and resource requests/limits quarterly against real traffic growth.
  • Dependency upgrades — regular scheduled Spring Boot/Spring Cloud version upgrades across all services (staggered, not all at once) to avoid falling far behind and facing a painful "big bang" upgrade later.

41.2 Versioning & Backward Compatibility Discipline

Since services deploy independently, Accounts Service v42 must be able to talk to Cards Service v38 (an older version, mid-rollout) without breaking — this means API changes must be backward compatible by default (add new optional fields, never remove/rename existing ones without a version bump per Section 29).

44. Interview Questions & Answers (Basic → Advanced → Scenario Based)

Q1. What is the difference between monolithic and microservices architecture?
A monolith is one deployable unit with all modules sharing a codebase and database. Microservices split the application into small, independently deployable services, each with its own database, communicating over the network.
Q2. When would you recommend staying with a monolith instead of moving to microservices?
For small teams, early-stage products with unclear domain boundaries, low traffic internal tools, or organizations lacking mature CI/CD and monitoring practices — the operational overhead of microservices isn't justified until there's a proven need for independent scaling or release cadence.
Q3. How does Eureka enable service discovery, and what happens if Eureka itself goes down?
Services register with Eureka and send heartbeats; clients query Eureka for healthy instances of a service name. Eureka clients cache the last known registry locally, so brief Eureka downtime doesn't immediately break service-to-service calls — but new service instances can't register until Eureka recovers.
Q4. Explain the Saga pattern and why it's needed.
Since microservices can't share a single ACID transaction across databases, a Saga breaks a business transaction into a sequence of local transactions per service, each with a compensating transaction to undo it if a later step fails — trading strict consistency for eventual consistency with guaranteed rollback.
Real scenario: Loan disbursement — if crediting the account fails after the loan was created, a compensating transaction cancels the loan record instead of leaving the system in an inconsistent state.
Q5. Difference between Choreography and Orchestration sagas?
Choreography is decentralized — each service reacts to events independently, no central coordinator. Orchestration has a dedicated coordinator service explicitly directing each step. Choreography suits simple flows; orchestration scales better for complex multi-step business processes.
Q6. What problem does a Circuit Breaker solve, and how does it differ from a simple try-catch?
A Circuit Breaker prevents cascading failures by tracking failure rates over a sliding window and "opening" to fail fast (skip the real call entirely) once a threshold is breached, giving the downstream service time to recover — a try-catch alone still makes the call and waits for it to fail/timeout every single time.
Q7. Why is idempotency important, and how do you implement it?
Network retries and at-least-once message delivery mean the same operation can be triggered more than once; idempotency ensures repeating it produces the same result instead of duplicate side effects (e.g., double payment). Implemented via an idempotency key that the server checks before processing.
Q8. Why does each microservice need its own database?
It preserves independent deployability and encapsulation — if services shared a database, a schema change for one service could break another, defeating the purpose of splitting into microservices. Cross-service data needs go through APIs or events, not direct queries.
Q9. How do you handle reporting/analytics that need data from multiple services?
Either API composition (call each service and merge results in code, fine for small-scale needs), or CQRS-style read models fed by domain events published to Kafka and projected into a dedicated reporting database optimized for queries.
Q10. What's the difference between REST and gRPC for inter-service communication, and when would you choose gRPC?
REST/JSON is simple, human-readable, and universally supported but slower; gRPC uses binary Protobuf over HTTP/2, much faster, ideal for high-throughput internal service-to-service calls where performance matters more than human readability (e.g., a fraud-scoring service called on every transaction).
Q11. How do you secure microservices end to end?
Authenticate once at the edge (API Gateway validates JWT issued by an OAuth2/OIDC provider like Keycloak), propagate the token to downstream services, and enforce authorization again at each service (defense in depth) rather than trusting internal network location as implicit trust.
Q12. How do you debug a slow request that spans multiple microservices?
Use distributed tracing (OpenTelemetry + Jaeger/Zipkin) to see the exact time spent in each service/span for that specific trace ID, then cross-reference centralized logs (ELK) filtered by the same trace/correlation ID to see detailed context for the slow span.
Q13. What is the Strangler Fig pattern and why use it over a full rewrite?
It incrementally extracts pieces of a monolith into microservices one module at a time, routing traffic gradually via a gateway, while the monolith keeps running — avoiding the high risk of a big-bang rewrite where the business can't ship features for a long freeze period.
Q14. How would you design the "close account" use case across Accounts, Cards, and Loans services?
Accounts Service synchronously checks (via REST/Feign) for active cards/loans before allowing closure; once closed, it publishes an AccountClosedEvent to Kafka, which Cards Service and Loans Service consume asynchronously to perform their own side effects (deactivate cards, flag loan records) — combining synchronous validation with asynchronous eventual-consistency side effects.
Q15. What are the biggest challenges you've faced with microservices in production, and how did you solve them?
Example: cascading failures from a slow downstream Card Network API — solved by adding Resilience4j circuit breaker + bulkhead + timeout, so a slow dependency degrades gracefully (fallback response) instead of exhausting Accounts Service's own thread pool and causing a full outage.
Q16. How do you deploy 20+ microservices without them stepping on each other?
Each service has its own independent CI/CD pipeline (scoped to its own repo/folder), its own Kubernetes Deployment/Service/HPA, and its own database — deployments are fully decoupled. Shared infrastructure (Gateway, Config Server, Eureka) is owned by a platform team and changed more conservatively.
Q17. What is API versioning and why is it necessary in microservices?
Since services deploy independently, older and newer versions of a service coexist during rollout, and external consumers (mobile apps) can't always upgrade instantly — versioning (typically URI path-based, e.g., /api/v2/) lets you evolve APIs without breaking existing consumers.
Q18. Explain the Bulkhead pattern with a real example.
Bulkhead isolates resources (e.g., thread pools) per downstream dependency so a slow/failing one doesn't exhaust resources needed for unrelated calls — e.g., giving Loans Service calls their own dedicated thread pool so a slowdown there doesn't also block Cards Service calls sharing the same caller.
Q19. How does Kubernetes complement microservices architecture?
It provides per-service independent scaling (HPA), self-healing (restarts/reschedules failed pods), rolling/canary/blue-green deployment support, built-in DNS-based service discovery, and namespace-based environment isolation — solving most of the operational challenges that come with running many small services.
Q20. What would you consider before splitting a monolith into microservices?
Whether bounded contexts are actually well understood (DDD analysis), whether the team has mature CI/CD/monitoring/containerization practices, whether there's a genuine need for independent scaling/release cadence per module, and a realistic incremental migration plan (Strangler Fig) rather than a risky big-bang rewrite.

45. Final Summary & Cheat Sheet

Monolith: one deployable unit, one database, simple to start with, but scales and releases as a whole.

Microservices: independently deployable services organized around business capabilities, each with its own database, communicating over REST/gRPC/events — powerful but operationally complex.

Our banking example: Accounts, Cards, and Loans services, each owning their data, communicating synchronously (Feign/REST) for immediate needs and asynchronously (Kafka) for decoupled side effects, protected by circuit breakers/retries/bulkheads, secured with JWT/OAuth2/Keycloak, observed via Actuator/Micrometer/Prometheus/Grafana/OpenTelemetry/Jaeger/ELK, and deployed independently via Azure DevOps to AKS.

The golden rule: don't adopt microservices because it's trendy — adopt it when bounded contexts are clear, teams need independent release cadence, and your organization has the DevOps maturity (CI/CD, containerization, observability) to operate a distributed system reliably.
